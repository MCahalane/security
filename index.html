The campus café · Fictional scenario

8:45 a.m. The line is growing.

The café’s online ordering screen stops working. A supplier’s bank details have changed unexpectedly. Your manager sends a voice message asking you to pay urgently. It sounds exactly like her. Is this a technology problem, a people problem, or a business problem?

What you should be able to do

  • Explain why information resources are vulnerable, using the chapter’s five factors.
  • Distinguish mistakes from deliberate attacks and recognize the ten threat categories.
  • Choose among risk acceptance, limitation and transference.
  • Explain physical, access and communications controls in business language.
  • Evaluate AI-related threats and propose a response and recovery plan.

Information security means protecting information and the systems that handle it against unauthorized access, change, disruption or loss. The assets include customer records, devices, software, staff knowledge and the processes that let a business operate.

ConfidentialityOnly the right people can see the information.

A stranger downloads the payroll file: confidentiality is lost.

IntegrityInformation remains accurate and changes are authorized.

A supplier’s bank account is altered: integrity is lost.

AvailabilityPeople can use information and services when needed.

The ordering system goes down at lunch: availability is lost.

These three objectives are often called the CIA triad; here, CIA has nothing to do with the U.S. intelligence agency. This is a useful contemporary organizing lens for the chapter. One incident can affect all three objectives.

A threat is not the same as a vulnerability

Imagine a café laptop containing unencrypted payroll data. Theft is the threat; leaving it unattended with weak protection is a vulnerability; exposed employee details and interruption are potential harm or exposure. Risk considers how likely that harm is and how serious it would be. A control, such as a locked cabinet, reduces risk.

Terminology bridge: the textbook describes vulnerability broadly as the possibility of harm. Modern security work often uses it more specifically for a weakness that a threat can exploit.

Why are businesses vulnerable? The chapter’s five factors

  1. Interconnection: the café depends on a payment provider, delivery app and cloud account. A supplier’s problem can become its problem.
  2. Portable, powerful devices: one lost phone can expose access to many business services.
  3. Lower barriers to attack: ready-made tools reduce the expertise needed; generative AI can help produce convincing messages.
  4. Organized cybercrime: criminals divide work, sell access and operate across borders.
  5. Weak management support: a manager who rewards speed while discouraging verification makes shortcuts predictable.
Carry Lesson 5 forward. Encryption may protect a customer database, but it does not make unnecessary collection or unfair profiling ethical. Security and privacy overlap; neither replaces the other.

Stage 237–50 min · Chapter 4.2 + cognitive security

Recognize manipulation

A staff member attaches the wrong payroll file to an email. That is an unintentional mistake. A criminal pretending to be a supplier persuades that person to send it. The criminal is carrying out a deliberate social engineering attack, even if the employee’s error is unintentional.

Social engineering is manipulation of people to obtain access, information or action. It exploits trust, urgency, authority and helpfulness. Better training matters, but so do workload, clear procedures, usable systems and managers who support staff when they pause.

Recognize the channel

Phishing: a deceptive message seeking a click, secret or action. Spear phishing: tailored to a particular target. Smishing: through text messages. Vishing: through voice calls or messages.

Perfect spelling does not prove legitimacy. AI can make a fraudulent message fluent and personal.

Recognize the situation

Tailgating: following an authorized person through a locked door. Shoulder surfing: observing a screen or keyboard. An impressive badge or familiar voice is a claim of identity, not reliable proof.

A helpful employee needs an easy way to verify visitors and report suspicious requests.

Human-centred security · Contemporary extension

Cognitive Security + Digital Wellness

Protect the attention and judgement that secure decisions depend on.

People are components of information systems. Hardware, software, data, networks and procedures work with people who interpret messages, approve changes and respond to problems. A technically secure account can still be compromised if someone is persuaded to approve an unexpected login.

Here, cognitive security is a human-centred lens on protecting attention, judgement and decision-making. It helps explain how the conditions around a person can affect information security. It is not a fourth CIA objective: confidentiality, integrity and availability remain the outcomes we protect.

1 · Recognise an everyday moment

For each card, decide what you would do before opening its debrief. Ask both “What can I change?” and “What should the system change?”

Attention switching

“Where was I?”

You’re checking an invoice between a group-chat reply, a lecture alert and a café shift message. You return to the invoice and skim past changed bank details.

Your decision: finish quickly, or pause and recheck the change?

Reveal the security connection

Switching tasks requires you to recover context. Cognitive load is the mental demand a task and its environment place on limited working-memory resources. The classroom risk is a missed discrepancy leading to an unauthorized payment change: an integrity problem.

Protect a short verification window; designers should highlight what changed, and managers should allow time for independent approval.

Security fatigue

“Just let me get on with it.”

After a series of routine prompts, another approval request arrives. You did not start this login. It looks much like the earlier prompts.

Your decision: approve to clear it, or deny and investigate?

Reveal the security connection

Repetition can encourage automatic responses; security fatigue describes weariness and disengagement around security demands. They are related ideas, not identical diagnoses. Approving an attacker’s login could expose private records (confidentiality) and permit changes or deletion (integrity/availability).

Deny unexpected requests and report through a known channel. Designers should reduce unnecessary prompts and distinguish consequential ones; organisations should provide usable authentication and support.

Persuasion & urgency

“Your account closes in ten minutes.”

A message says your student account will be suspended unless you enter your password now. It uses the university logo and a convincing tone.

Your decision: use its link, or open the university service independently?

Reveal the security connection

Urgency and apparent authority can narrow deliberation. This is social engineering; stolen credentials threaten confidentiality and may enable unauthorized changes or lockout.

Use a saved or independently verified service address. Institutions should make legitimate alerts recognisable and offer a simple reporting route without punishing someone who asks for help.

AI reliance

“The assistant checked it.”

An AI summary says a supplier’s new account is safe because the invoice looks professional. You are tempted to accept the polished answer.

Your decision: accept its confidence, or identify what it actually verified?

Reveal the security connection

Cognitive offloading means using tools to carry part of a mental task; notes, reminders and AI can help. Over-reliance arises when assistance replaces necessary judgement. A fluent summary is not evidence of payment authority: an integrity risk. Uploading confidential records to an unapproved AI service also risks confidentiality.

Check original evidence and permissions. Designers should expose sources and uncertainty; organisations should define approved tools and enforce payment boundaries outside the AI.

3 · Follow the chain—and find a place to intervene

Environment: a message threatens to close your account. Intervention: institutions provide trusted reporting and clear account notices.

Read all four chains and intervention points
  • Urgency: threatening message → time pressure → enter credentials through its link → account disclosure or lockout. Intervene with a trusted reporting route, a pause and independent verification.
  • Switching: many notifications → attention shifts and context must be recovered → miss an invoice discrepancy → unauthorized change to payment data (integrity). Intervene with quiet verification time, visible change summaries and a second approver.
  • Repetition: similar prompts → familiarity and weariness → approve an unexpected login → exposed records and unauthorized changes. Intervene with fewer unnecessary prompts, clear login context, denial/reporting and phishing-resistant authentication where feasible.
  • AI: a confident recommendation → assumed authority → accept without evidence → incorrect records or payment decisions (integrity). Intervene with source access, uncertainty cues, independent checks and limited AI permissions.

These are possible pathways, not inevitable reactions. A technical control can interrupt the chain even when a person makes a mistake.

6 · Digital wellness is part of the working conditions

Digital wellness means shaping technology use to support wellbeing and sustainable functioning. Here, its security role is to help preserve cognitive capacity for decisions. It complements technical controls; it is not a promise that a rested person cannot be deceived.

You

Pause with a purpose

Set a short focus window for consequential checks. Batch optional notifications. Take a break or ask for a second review when overloaded. Verify urgent requests independently and report mistakes promptly.

Designers

Make secure choices usable

Reduce redundant prompts. Explain the action and consequence plainly. Use safe defaults, accessible warning design, visible changes and easy cancel/report paths. Show AI evidence and uncertainty.

Organisations

Make pausing possible

Provide realistic workloads, protected breaks, clear approval routes and approved tools. Support reporting without blame. Measure whether controls work in practice, not just whether staff completed training.

Shared responsibility: attackers are responsible for their deception. People deserve systems that remain protective under distraction, fatigue, disability and pressure. “Be more careful” is not an adequate control.

Stage 312–15 min · Chapter 4.3

Connect attacks to consequences

Attack names are useful only if you can connect them to a business consequence. Start with the asset: money, customer trust, a critical service, intellectual property or physical operations.

Malware, explained without the mystery

Malware is software designed to do harm. A virus attaches to another program or file; a worm can spread between systems without needing to attach to a host file. A Trojan disguises harmful functionality as something legitimate. Spyware secretly gathers information. These labels can overlap.

Ransomware can lock files or systems and demand payment. Criminals may also steal data and threaten publication: backups help restore availability but do not undo stolen information. Denial of service overwhelms a service so legitimate users cannot use it; a distributed attack uses many sources. Think of an ordering counter flooded with fake orders.

Chapter 4’s ten deliberate-threat categories, with business examples
  1. Espionage or trespass: unauthorized access to a competitor’s confidential pricing. Reading its public website is different.
  2. Information extortion: a criminal demands money to avoid releasing customer records.
  3. Sabotage or vandalism: deliberately damaging a storefront website or deleting records.
  4. Theft of equipment or information: taking a laptop or copying a confidential file without authority.
  5. Identity theft: assuming another person’s identity to open accounts or obtain access.
  6. Compromises to intellectual property: unauthorized copying of protected software, designs or business material.
  7. Software attacks: malware, denial of service, hidden backdoors and logic bombs. A logic bomb activates harmful behavior when a condition is met; a backdoor bypasses normal access checks.
  8. Alien software: the textbook’s term for software installed covertly or deceptively. Examples include spyware and unwanted advertising software; not every cookie or ad-supported application is malicious.
  9. SCADA attacks: interference with systems that monitor or control physical equipment, such as water pumps or factory machinery. SCADA means supervisory control and data acquisition.
  10. Cyberterrorism and cyberwarfare: cyber operations intended to intimidate or pursue conflict-related objectives. Do not infer an actor’s identity or motive just from an outage.

Categories overlap. A ransomware incident can involve software attacks, theft and extortion at the same time.

Stage 412–15 min · Chapter 4.4

Make a defensible risk decision

“Buy the best security” is not a usable business plan. A manager must identify what matters, estimate possible harm, choose controls, name an owner and review whether the approach works.

1 · AnalyzeWhat assets and services matter? What threats and weaknesses could harm them? How likely and severe is the harm?
2 · TreatChoose a response, assign responsibility and fund it. Plan for prevention and recovery.
3 · EvaluateTest the controls, inspect evidence and review the risk left over.

The chapter’s three strategies

Accept

Knowingly live with a risk and absorb possible loss. The café accepts a short interruption to its optional digital menu because printed menus are available. Acceptance needs an owner and review date; ignoring a risk is not a reasoned decision.

Limit

Reduce the chance or impact of harm. Protect accounts, restrict permissions, verify bank changes and test backups. This is also called risk reduction.

Transfer

Use insurance or contractual arrangements to shift some financial consequences. Coverage conditions matter. A policy does not prevent an attack or transfer all responsibility and reputational damage.

A modern extension: avoid

Do not undertake an unnecessarily risky activity. The café decides its AI assistant will not make supplier payments. Avoidance is an extra option here, not one of the textbook’s three named strategies.

A simple risk estimate

Fictional teaching model: one defined incident could cost US$50,000. A proposed control costs US$2,000 per year and is assumed to halve its annual probability. Change the assumed starting probability.

Expected loss = annual probability × loss if the incident occurs. This simplified model assumes one incident at most and a fixed loss. Real estimates are uncertain; legal duties, safety, severe tail risks and trust may outweigh this calculation.

Residual risk is what remains after controls. A good decision records both the assumptions and what would make the organization revisit them.

Stage 518–22 min · Chapter 4.5 + AI extension

Build layers—and bound the AI

Defense in depth means using several controls so that one failure does not expose everything. Think of a hotel: reception checks identity, a room key opens selected doors, alarms detect trouble and an evacuation plan handles emergencies.

Physical controls

Locks, visitor checks, protected server rooms, fire detection and backup power. At the café, secure the office and protect the device used for payroll. Security includes floods, fire and accidental damage.

Access controls

Authentication: prove who you are. Authorization: determine what you may do. A cashier may log in successfully but still have no right to change supplier accounts.

Least privilege: grant only the access needed for the task. Review it when people change roles or leave.

Recall Stage 2: repeated approvals can invite automatic responses. Pair usable MFA with clear login context, easy denial/reporting and support when someone receives an unexpected request. A focus habit complements these controls.

More than a password

Multifactor authentication (MFA) combines different types of evidence, such as something you know and something you have. Two passwords are still one factor type. A stolen password is less useful when another factor is required.

Some MFA can still be phished or defeated through account recovery. Phishing-resistant approaches, such as appropriately configured security keys or passkeys, make fake-login attacks harder. Managers should ask whether privileged accounts and recovery procedures receive the same protection.

Sources: Australian Signals Directorate · Implementing MFA

Communications controls: protect movement and boundaries

  • Firewall: applies rules to network traffic, like a gate checking permitted routes. It cannot decide whether every allowed business request is honest.
  • Anti-malware: detects or blocks harmful software. It needs maintenance and does not catch every threat.
  • Allowlisting / blocklisting: permit approved software or block known unwanted software. The textbook uses “whitelisting” and “blacklisting.”
  • Encryption: transforms readable data into a form requiring a key to recover it. Protect the keys too. Authorized access to decrypted data can still be abused.
  • HTTPS / TLS: protects a connection to a website. A scam site can also use HTTPS; the connection’s protection does not establish the business’s honesty. TLS is the modern term; SSL is its obsolete predecessor.
  • VPN: an encrypted tunnel across a network. It does not make every destination safe or an infected device trustworthy.
  • Monitoring and logs: records and alerts help detect suspicious actions. Connect this to Lesson 5: monitoring should be proportionate, access-controlled and explained.
Optional textbook bridge: keys and digital certificates

Symmetric encryption uses a shared secret key; asymmetric encryption uses a linked public and private key. A digital certificate helps bind a public key to an identity, such as a website domain. A digital signature can support checking origin and detecting changes. None of these proves that a business claim is true. Managers need appropriate implementation and key management, not memorized mathematics.

AI introduces a new boundary problem

A generative AI model produces content from patterns learned during training. An AI agent combines a model with tools that can take actions, such as reading email or updating a customer record. Tool permissions determine how much damage a bad decision can cause.

Fictional illustration · Data is not authority

The invoice that tries to become the boss

A café assistant reads a supplier invoice. Inside it is text telling the assistant to ignore its approved task and change payment details. The invoice is outside content to inspect; it is not authorized to issue instructions.

Untrusted inputEmail, invoice or web page contains an attacker’s request.
AI decisionThe system may confuse content it reads with an instruction it should follow.
Business actionOverbroad tool permissions could turn the mistake into a changed record or leaked file.

This is indirect prompt injection: an attempt to redirect an AI system through material it consumes. It differs from a hallucination, an unsupported or false generated output that need not involve an attacker. Both can harm a business, but they have different causes.

Better design: separate outside content from authorized instructions; restrict accessible data and tools; require independently verified human approval for sensitive changes; log actions; test with adversarial examples; and provide a stop mechanism. A sentence telling the model to “be safe” is not an enforced permission boundary.

AI can help defenders too—but who checks the helper?

AI can help summarize security logs, group suspicious messages and draft incident updates. A person still needs to check the evidence and approve consequential actions. Stage 618–22 min · Chapter 4.5 + application

Keep operating and recover

Security is incomplete if the plan ends at “stop attackers.” A business also needs to notice trouble, contain harm, keep essential work going and restore trustworthy systems.

Continuity and recovery are connected

Business continuity keeps essential services operating during disruption. Disaster recovery restores technology and data. The textbook uses the terms closely; modern practice often distinguishes the broader business plan from its technology recovery component.

For the café, continuity might mean a safe, approved manual ordering process. Recovery means restoring verified records and systems. Do not improvise by writing down customers’ card numbers. A working backup is useful only if it is protected from the same incident and can actually be restored.

How long can we wait?

Recovery time objective (RTO): target time to restore a service. “Ordering should return within two hours.”

How much data can we lose?

Recovery point objective (RPO): target maximum data loss measured in time. “We should lose no more than 15 minutes of orders.” These are planning targets, not guarantees.

Chapter bridge: hot, warm and cold recovery sites

A hot site has systems ready for rapid use; a warm site has some infrastructure but requires additional setup; a cold site provides basic facilities and needs much more preparation. Greater readiness generally costs more. Cloud recovery changes the implementation, but managers still trade cost against restoration time and data loss.

The first response: calm, evidence-based decisions

  1. Report and activate: contact the designated incident lead through an established channel.
  2. Contain: authorized responders limit affected accounts, connections or tools while considering operational consequences.
  3. Preserve and assess: retain relevant logs and messages; determine what is known and what remains uncertain.
  4. Continue and communicate: use approved fallback processes. Give accurate updates and coordinate any required notifications with the responsible specialists.
  5. Recover and learn: restore trusted systems, test them, monitor for recurrence and address the underlying weaknesses.

Managers should avoid promising that “no data was stolen” before evidence supports it. A quick acknowledgment can be honest without guessing at scope.

A current management lens: NIST’s Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 organizes outcomes into Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover. “Govern” makes ownership, policy and oversight explicit. These functions work together; they are not a one-time checklist.

Sources: NIST · Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, February 26, 2024 · FTC · Cybersecurity for Small Business

Audit: show that a control works

An information systems audit examines controls and evidence. A written policy is not proof of implementation. Ask for an access review, a backup restoration result, an exception log or a sample of independently approved payments.

Chapter bridge: three audit approaches

Around the computer: check known inputs against expected outputs. Through the computer: examine processing and logic as well. With the computer: use software to help analyze records and test controls. Internal and external auditors bring different organizational positions; both need appropriate objectivity and evidence.

Cognitive security: evidence and limits

New source and video URLs checked October 1, 2026 through publisher, author or indexed video records. Talks provide discussion prompts; research below supports narrower claims. None of these studies establishes that this lesson or its habit-builder reduces breach rates. “Cognitive security” is used here as a teaching lens, not a newly standardised CIA category.

  1. Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert (2015), The Attentional Cost of Receiving a Cell Phone Notification. Notifications disrupted performance on an attention-demanding experimental task even without phone interaction. Application: protect attention during verification. The study did not measure invoice errors or cyber incidents.
  2. Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008), The Cost of Interrupted Work: More Speed and Stress. In this experiment, interrupted work was completed faster without a quality difference, but with higher reported stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. Do not claim every interruption inevitably slows work or causes errors. Application: examine workload and switching costs around security tasks.
  3. Stanton, Theofanos, Spickard Prettyman & Furman (2016), Security Fatigue. A qualitative interview study (40 participants) identified fatigue in participants’ accounts of security decisions. It provides insight into experiences, not a population prevalence estimate or proof of a specific causal chain. Application: reduce unnecessary security decisions and support users.
  4. Fitz et al. (2019), Batching smartphone notifications can improve well-being. A randomised field study examined scheduled notification delivery and reported benefits including reduced stress in the batching condition. Application: try batching nonessential messages. The result does not establish that all alerts should be disabled or that batching prevents attacks.
  5. Castelo et al. (2025), Blocking mobile internet on smartphones improves sustained attention, mental health, and subjective well-being. A randomised trial tested a two-week mobile-internet block while allowing calls, texts and internet use on other devices; sustained attention and wellbeing improved. Adherence was difficult: 119 of 467 initial participants met the preregistered compliance threshold. Application: consider feasible boundaries rather than prescribe a universal “detox.” Brief focus windows are a classroom adaptation, not the tested intervention, and security outcomes were not measured.
  6. Lee et al. (2025), The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking. A survey of knowledge workers studied self-reported thinking and confidence during AI use. These reports do not demonstrate long-term cognitive decline or prove that AI caused weaker skills. Application: ask what evidence remains unchecked when delegating part of a task.

Quick vocabulary

Asset: something the business values and needs to protect

Threat: a potential cause of harm

Vulnerability: a weakness or susceptibility that can allow harm

Risk: likelihood and consequence of harm

Control: a measure to reduce risk

Residual risk: risk remaining after controls

Authentication: checking identity

Authorization: deciding permitted access or action

Least privilege: only the permissions required for a task

Social engineering: manipulating people into an unsafe disclosure or action

Deepfake: synthetic media imitating a real person

Prompt injection: an attempt to redirect an AI system through instructions it should not trust

Business continuity: keeping essential operations going during disruption

Disaster recovery: restoring technology and data after disruption
